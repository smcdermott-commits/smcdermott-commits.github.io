## Project Overview

In Fall 2026, I built and flew two high-power rockets with the **Mudd Amateur Rocketry Club (MARC)** to earn my **National Association of Rocketry (NAR) Level 1 certification**. I followed the club's established rocket design and build guidance, taking responsibility for fabricating and assembling the vehicles, preparing their recovery systems, and flying them at the launch site.

## Build & Launch Responsibilities

- **Fabrication:** 3D-printed and laser-cut components according to the club's design.
- **Assembly:** Built up the airframe with extensive epoxy work.
- **Launch preparation:** Adjusted motor ejection timing at the launch site and packed the recovery system.
- **Flight and recovery:** Prepared the rockets for flight, then recovered the vehicle after launch.

## Flight 1 — *Going Bananas*

My first rocket, *Going Bananas*, flew in October but experienced a recovery-system failure. The shock cord broke during flight. The body tube fell near the launch site, while the nosecone—still attached to the lightweight parachute—drifted away and was not recovered. The airframe was damaged beyond reflight, so this attempt did not count toward certification.

We traced the likely cause to too much protective cellulose insulation (“dog barf”) around the ejection charge. The excess insulation appears to have reflected heat back toward the shock cord, burning through it. The failure highlighted how sensitive recovery-system reliability can be to material choices and small packing details.

## Rebuild & Flight 2 — *Thrust Fund Baby*

I started rebuilding as soon as we returned from the launch, beginning with 3D-printed replacement components. For *Thrust Fund Baby*, I used a tougher shock cord and a more appropriate amount of protective insulation around the ejection charge.

The second rocket flew successfully and was fully recovered, earning me my **NAR Level 1 high-power certification**.

## What I Learned

- **Recovery-system margins matter:** Small decisions about shock-cord strength and ejection-charge insulation can determine whether a rocket is recovered intact.
- **Failure analysis drives iteration:** Identifying a likely failure mechanism made it possible to make targeted changes for the next flight.
- **Fast iteration benefits from accessible fabrication:** Having access to 3D printing and club resources let me begin rebuilding immediately.
- **Build execution is its own engineering challenge:** Even when following an established design, fabrication quality, assembly, and launch preparation all require care.
